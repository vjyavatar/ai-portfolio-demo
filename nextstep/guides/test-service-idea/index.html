THE NEXTSTEP FIELD GUIDE

Test a service idea in seven days

The purpose of a first week is to learn whether your idea is useful. A finished website or enthusiastic compliment does not establish demand.

Days 1–2: define and demonstrate

Choose one audience, one recurring problem, and one small result. Create a sample using fictional information. Make the result easy to judge: a clearer document, a usable checklist, or a learner completing a task independently.

Days 3–4: have useful conversations

Ask people who experience the problem how they handled it last time. Listen before presenting your idea. Avoid asking only whether they “like” it.

  • When did this last happen?
  • What did you do, and what was difficult?
  • What would a successful improvement look like?
  • Who decides whether to spend time or money on solving it?

Days 5–6: agree and deliver

If there is a fit, propose a bounded pilot. State what is included, what is excluded, when it will be ready, how it will be reviewed, and whether it is paid or voluntary. Only accept work you are qualified and permitted to perform.

Day 7: examine the evidence

Record conversations, completed pilots, and requests to work together again. Keep notes on delivery time, costs, and what needed revision. A repeat request is encouraging, but you still need to examine whether the work is sustainable.

Decide your next experiment

  • No responses: revisit the audience and how you describe the problem.
  • Interest without commitment: ask what prevents someone from proceeding. Reduce uncertainty around the scope.
  • A useful pilot: repeat it before expanding. Find out which parts are consistently valuable.

A seven-day plan is a flexible schedule, not a promise that customers will arrive in a week. Continue at a pace that fits your circumstances.
